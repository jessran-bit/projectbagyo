Round 1 Lesson • 3 mins
What is a Project Charter?
Before any project officially starts, someone in authority must say: "Yes, this project is approved. Here is what it is, how long it takes, and how much we're spending." That document is the Project Charter.

Think of it like the government issuing a mandate: "DOST authorizes PhilSpace to build a climate satellite, with a budget of PHP 2.8 Billion, to be done within 18 months." Without that document, no one has the authority to spend money or assign people.

The charter also sets constraints -- what is fixed versus what can flex. Imagine three dials: Cost, Time, and Scope. For any given project, one dial is locked (Constrain), one can bend a little (Accept), and one you actually want to push further if possible (Enhance). You cannot lock all three -- something always has to give.
Real-world example The Metro Manila Subway was given a fixed government budget (Cost = Constrain) and a flexible scope (fewer stations is okay if needed), while the timeline is something they want to beat if possible (Time = Enhance).
✦The Project Manager (PM) is the person responsible for delivering the mission. Pick one team member for this role.
✦The Sponsor is the executive or agency who approved and funded it. Your Scenario Card names them.
✦Feasibility means asking: before we commit, is this actually doable? You check Technical (can it be built?), Financial (can we afford it?), Schedule (can we do it in time?), and Political/Legal (do we have permission?).

Round 2A Lesson • 2 mins
What is a Work Breakdown Structure (WBS)?
A WBS is how you take a big project and break it down into smaller, manageable pieces of work. Think of it like a family tree, but for work. At the top is the whole project. Below that are the major deliverables -- the big outputs you will produce. Below those are work packages -- the specific tasks assigned to a team.

The key rule: every item in the WBS must be a real deliverable or task of the project. Things like "team lunch" or "office renovation" are not satellite mission deliverables -- they do not belong in the WBS.
How to tell them apart Level 1 - Major Deliverable: The big output. Examples: "Satellite Assembly" (you will have a finished satellite), "Ground Station Setup" (you will have a working ground station), "Mission Operations" (you will have an ongoing ops capability).

Level 2 - Work Package: A specific task. Examples: "Payload Integration" (bolting sensors into the satellite), "Software Development" (writing the control software), "Antenna Installation" (physical antenna work).

Round 2B Lesson • 2 mins
What is Cost Estimation and Budgeting?
Once you know WHAT needs to be done (the WBS), you need to figure out HOW MUCH each piece costs. This is called cost estimation. Add all the estimates together and you get the project budget.

In real projects, costs are not known exactly in advance -- they are ranges. You pick a number somewhere in that range based on your best judgment. The goal is to stay within your approved budget. Underestimating is risky (you run out of money). Overestimating wastes resources that could go elsewhere.
How to approach this Each valid tile shows a cost range in PHP Millions. Example: "Payload Integration: PHP 280M - 340M." You decide where in that range to estimate -- PHP 280M (conservative), PHP 310M (midpoint), or PHP 340M (high end). Your total across all 8 valid tiles should ideally stay within your mission's approved budget.
✦Only budget the tiles you marked as VALID in Round 2A. Discarded tiles are not in scope.
✦Enter amounts in PHP Millions. If the range is PHP 280M-340M, enter a number between 280 and 340.

Round 3 Lesson • 2 mins
What is Risk and Project Change Management?
No project goes exactly as planned. Unexpected events -- called risks or anomalies -- happen. When they do, the Project Manager has three basic options:
ACCEPT
Deal with it as-is. Take the hit -- whether that's extra cost, a delay, or reduced scope. Sometimes this is the fastest path forward, but it may set a bad precedent.
NEGOTIATE
Find a middle ground. This is almost always the best project management answer -- you reduce the impact by sharing it, delaying it partially, or finding a creative alternative that costs less than full acceptance.
REJECT
Push back and refuse the change or demand. Sometimes justified, but often risky -- it doesn't make the problem go away and can damage relationships with stakeholders.
Scoring tip You earn MORE points if your justification names a specific PHP amount (e.g. "this saves PHP 40M compared to the ACCEPT option") and identifies which constraint is affected (Cost, Time, or Scope). Vague answers like "it's the best choice" earn fewer points.
